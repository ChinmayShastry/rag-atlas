The Craft and Chemistry of Coffee Roasting

Green coffee is, to the untrained eye, an unpromising material. The seeds arrive pale and dense, smelling faintly of hay and raw peanuts, and they carry roughly ten to twelve percent moisture by weight. Nothing about them suggests the aromatic complexity locked inside. Roasting is the process that unlocks it, and it is best understood not as cooking but as a carefully steered chain of chemical reactions driven by heat applied over time.

A roast begins with the charge. The roaster preheats the drum to a target temperature, often somewhere between 180 and 220 degrees Celsius depending on batch size and machine design, and then drops the green coffee in. The mass of cool beans immediately pulls heat out of the drum, and the temperature probe plunges. The low point of that plunge is called the turnaround point, and it usually arrives about sixty to ninety seconds after the charge. From there the bean mass begins to climb, and the roaster's job for the next eight to fourteen minutes is to manage the rate of that climb.

The first phase is the drying phase. Water must leave the bean before meaningful browning can occur, because evaporation holds the bean surface near the boiling point of water and caps the temperature at which other reactions can proceed. This phase typically occupies the first four to six minutes and ends somewhere near 150 degrees Celsius. Roasters listen and look for the color shift from green to pale yellow, and many describe a smell like toasted grain or basmati rice at this stage.

Once the free moisture is gone, browning begins in earnest. Two families of reactions dominate. The Maillard reaction couples amino acids with reducing sugars, generating hundreds of volatile aromatic compounds and the brown melanoidin pigments that give roasted coffee its color. Caramelization, a separate process, breaks down sugars under heat without requiring amino acids. Together these reactions build the bulk of what a drinker later perceives as body, sweetness, and roast character. Because they are time and temperature dependent, two roasts that finish at identical temperatures but take different paths to get there will taste noticeably different.

Around 196 degrees Celsius, or roughly 385 degrees Fahrenheit, the bean reaches first crack. Steam and carbon dioxide generated inside the bean build pressure until the cellular structure fractures audibly. The sound resembles popcorn, sharp and irregular, and it marks the point at which coffee becomes drinkable. The bean roughly doubles in volume, its surface smooths, and the silverskin, or chaff, sloughs off and is drawn away by the roaster's cyclone.

What happens after first crack is where roasters exercise the most judgment. The interval between the onset of first crack and the moment the coffee is dropped from the drum is called development time. Expressed as a fraction of total roast length, it is known as the development time ratio, and it commonly falls between fifteen and twenty-five percent. Too short a development produces sour, grassy, underdeveloped cups with a papery finish. Too long flattens acidity and pushes the coffee toward generic roast flavors that mask origin character.

If heat continues to be applied, the coffee reaches second crack at approximately 224 degrees Celsius, or about 435 degrees Fahrenheit. This sound is quieter and more continuous than first crack, a crackling rather than a popping, and it is caused by the fracturing of the bean's cell walls as oils migrate to the surface. Roasts taken well into second crack develop the dark, glossy, bittersweet profile associated with traditional espresso blends and French roasts. Past that point the coffee moves quickly toward carbonization, and the risk of a drum fire rises sharply.

Weight loss is a reliable index of roast degree. A light roast typically sheds twelve to fourteen percent of its green weight, most of it water. A medium roast loses fifteen to seventeen percent. Dark roasts can shed eighteen to twenty percent or more, at which point a substantial portion of the loss is organic matter rather than moisture. Because the bean expands while losing mass, roasted coffee is dramatically less dense than green coffee, which is why volumetric dosing is unreliable and roasters and baristas alike work by weight.

Several common beliefs about roasting are worth correcting. Caffeine is relatively heat stable and does not burn off in any meaningful quantity during a normal roast. Dark roasts contain slightly less caffeine per bean than light roasts, but only because the bean has lost mass; measured by weight, the difference is small, and measured by scoop, dark roast can actually deliver more caffeine because the beans are less dense. What does change substantially is the chlorogenic acid content, which degrades steadily with heat and contributes to the shift from bright, tart acidity toward the softer bitterness of darker roasts.

The two commercially dominant species behave differently in the drum. Arabica, which accounts for the majority of specialty coffee, contains more lipids and sugars, roasts more delicately, and expresses more floral and fruit character. Robusta carries roughly twice the caffeine, more chlorogenic acid, and less sugar, giving it a heavier, more rubbery profile and a thick crema prized in some espresso traditions. Density also matters: high-grown beans from elevations above 1,500 meters are harder and denser, absorb heat more slowly, and generally tolerate more aggressive early heat application than soft, low-grown beans.

Roasting does not end when the coffee leaves the drum. Freshly roasted beans are saturated with carbon dioxide produced during pyrolysis, and this gas must escape before the coffee brews predictably. The process is called degassing. Excess carbon dioxide interferes with water contact during extraction, causing dramatic blooming and uneven, often sour results. Most filter coffees settle after three to seven days of rest. Espresso, which relies on sustained pressure and shorter contact time, is generally judged to improve for seven to fourteen days after roast, and some roasters recommend even longer for dark roasts.

Storage governs how long that peak lasts. The enemies of roasted coffee are oxygen, moisture, heat, and light, in roughly that order of importance. A one-way valve bag allows carbon dioxide to vent while blocking oxygen from entering, which is why quality roasters use them. Freezing green or roasted coffee in genuinely airtight containers can preserve it for months, but repeated freeze-thaw cycles drive condensation into the porous bean structure and cause rapid staling. Grinding accelerates every one of these processes by multiplying the exposed surface area, which is the single strongest practical argument for grinding immediately before brewing.
