Heat Treating Carbon Steel

Steel is unusual among common materials in that its properties can be changed dramatically without changing its composition at all. The same bar can be made soft enough to file or hard enough to scratch glass, purely through a sequence of heating and cooling. What makes this possible is that iron rearranges its crystal structure at a specific temperature, and how quickly it is cooled through that rearrangement determines what it becomes.

Below roughly seven hundred and twenty-three degrees Celsius, carbon steel exists as ferrite and cementite, a structure in which carbon sits in distinct iron carbide regions. Above that temperature, known as the lower critical point, the iron converts to austenite, a different crystal arrangement that can dissolve carbon throughout its structure. For most plain carbon steels the smith heats to somewhere between eight hundred and eight hundred and seventy degrees to ensure the conversion is complete and the carbon is evenly distributed.

Judging that temperature is a practical problem, and the traditional solution exploits a coincidence of physics. Iron loses its magnetism at its Curie point, around seven hundred and seventy degrees, which sits very close to the temperature range of interest. A smith touches a magnet to the heated steel; when the magnet no longer sticks, the steel is at or above the Curie point and is very likely austenitic. Colour is the other traditional guide, though it is badly misleading in bright daylight, and most smiths judge colour in deliberately dim conditions.

Quenching is the step that produces hardness. Steel cooled slowly from austenite has time for the carbon to migrate back out and reform its usual structure, and the result is soft. Cooled fast enough, the carbon is trapped in place and the iron transforms instead into martensite, a strained crystal structure that is extremely hard and correspondingly brittle. The quench medium is chosen to match the steel: brine and water pull heat out fastest and risk cracking, oil is gentler and suits most tool steels, and some alloys harden adequately in still air.

The risk during quenching is that the transformation involves a volume change, and the outside of the piece transforms before the inside. A blade quenched edge-first, or a piece with a sharp internal corner, can develop stresses large enough to crack it outright. Cracks from quenching are usually clean and follow the geometry of the part, appearing at the thickest-to-thinnest transitions where the cooling rate differed most.

Steel straight from the quench is almost never usable. Fully hardened martensite will hold an edge but will also chip or snap under moderate impact. Tempering restores toughness by reheating the piece to a much lower temperature, typically between one hundred and fifty and three hundred and fifty degrees, and holding it there. This allows a small controlled amount of the trapped carbon to precipitate out, relieving the internal strain. The tradeoff is direct and unavoidable: every degree of tempering temperature buys toughness at the cost of hardness.

The temperature of a temper can be read from the colour of the oxide film that forms on clean steel. A pale straw colour appears near two hundred and thirty degrees and suits a knife edge or a razor. Bronze and purple follow. A full blue, near three hundred degrees, indicates a considerably softer and tougher condition appropriate for springs and for tools that must absorb shock. These colours are a thin interference film, not a change in the metal itself, and they can be polished away without undoing the temper.

Two further treatments serve different purposes. Annealing means heating to the austenitic range and then cooling as slowly as possible, often by burying the piece in ash or shutting it in the forge overnight, which produces the softest and most machinable condition. Normalising means heating to the same range and then cooling in still air, which refines the grain structure and relieves stresses from forging without making the steel fully soft. Smiths commonly normalise two or three times after forging and before hardening, because forging leaves the grain coarse and uneven.

Overheating is the most damaging mistake available. Held far above the critical range, the grains of austenite grow, and coarse grain persists through the quench into the finished piece, leaving it brittle in a way that tempering cannot fix. Held hotter still, in an oxygen-rich atmosphere, carbon burns out of the surface layer entirely, a condition called decarburisation, which produces a piece that will not harden at its surface no matter how correctly it is quenched. Both faults are invisible until the work is finished and fails.
