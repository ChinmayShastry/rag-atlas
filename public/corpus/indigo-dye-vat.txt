The Indigo Vat

Indigo behaves unlike almost every other dye, and the whole practice of indigo dyeing follows from one inconvenient property: in its blue form the pigment is completely insoluble in water. It cannot simply be dissolved and applied. It must first be chemically converted into a different, soluble form, taken up by the fibre in that state, and then converted back. The colour a dyer wants is created after the cloth leaves the liquid, not while it is in it.

The conversion is a reduction. Blue indigotin, treated in an alkaline solution with something that will strip oxygen from it, becomes leuco-indigo, a form that dissolves readily and is not blue at all but a pale greenish yellow. A working vat therefore looks nothing like its product. Beneath the surface the liquid is yellow-green and translucent, and this appearance is the dyer's first indication that the vat is functioning.

Three conditions must hold at once. The solution must be alkaline, usually somewhere around pH ten to eleven, because leuco-indigo is only stable in alkaline conditions. It must contain an active reducing agent to remove oxygen and keep removing it. And it should be warm, since both the chemistry and, in traditional vats, the organisms driving it slow markedly when cold; fermentation vats are commonly held near fifty degrees Celsius.

How that reduction is achieved divides the craft. A fermentation vat uses microorganisms, fed with something they can metabolise such as bran, madder root, dates, or fruit sugars. The bacteria consume the sugars and, in doing so, consume the dissolved oxygen and drive the reduction. Such a vat takes one to two weeks to establish, must be fed to stay alive, and can be maintained for months or years. A chemical vat instead uses a reducing agent such as sodium dithionite, often called sodium hydrosulfite, and is ready within an hour. The chemical vat is faster and more predictable; the fermentation vat is gentler on fibres and, in the view of many dyers, produces better colour.

A healthy vat announces itself visually. The surface develops a coppery, iridescent scum, known as the flower or the bloom, where indigo in contact with the air has oxidised back to its blue form. A vat with a strong flower and a clear yellow-green body is working. A vat that has gone entirely blue throughout has lost its reduction and is described as sick; it can usually be revived by adding more reducing agent and warmth rather than being discarded.

The dyeing itself is deliberate and slow. Cloth is wetted first, so the dye penetrates evenly, then lowered into the vat with as little disturbance as possible. Every bubble introduced brings oxygen, which oxidises indigo prematurely and depletes the vat. For the same reason the dyer stirs gently and in one direction, and lifts the cloth out slowly rather than pulling it.

What follows is the part that surprises people watching for the first time. Cloth emerging from the vat is yellow-green. As it hangs in the air it turns, over perhaps thirty seconds to a few minutes, through a dull green and then to blue, as atmospheric oxygen converts the leuco form back to insoluble indigotin, now trapped inside the fibre. The colour is not applied to the cloth so much as precipitated within it.

Depth of shade is built by repetition, not by concentration. A single dip yields a pale blue however strong the vat is, because only a limited amount of dye can be taken up at once. Dyers therefore dip repeatedly, oxidising fully between each immersion, and it is common to use eight, twelve, or twenty dips to reach a deep navy. Each layer is thin, which is why worn indigo denim fades in exactly the places it is abraded most: the outer layers are being removed one at a time.

Because the pigment sits in layers on and near the fibre surface rather than bonded evenly throughout, indigo crocks, meaning it rubs off onto other surfaces. Careful rinsing in water, and often a final wash in a mildly acidic solution to neutralise remaining alkali, removes the loosest material. Some transfer nonetheless remains characteristic of the dye and is not regarded as a fault.

Fibre choice constrains the vat more than most dyers expect. Cellulose fibres such as cotton, linen, and hemp tolerate the alkalinity well. Protein fibres, wool and silk, do not; sustained exposure above roughly pH ten damages them, leaving wool harsh and weakened. Dyers working with protein fibres therefore keep the vat at the lower end of the workable range, accept slower uptake, and limit how long each dip lasts.
