Firing Ceramics: Kilns, Cones, and Atmosphere

Everything a potter does at the wheel is provisional until the work is fired. Firing is the irreversible step that converts a fragile, water-soluble clay body into a permanent ceramic material, and it is governed less by intuition than by a set of well-documented physical transformations that occur at predictable points along the temperature curve.

Most studio work passes through the kiln twice. The first pass is the bisque firing. Its purpose is to drive off chemically bound water and burn out organic material, converting the raw clay into a porous but durable state that can be handled and glazed without dissolving. Bisque is typically fired to Orton cone 06 through cone 04, corresponding roughly to 999 to 1063 degrees Celsius. The resulting body is strong enough to survive dipping yet still absorbent enough to draw water out of a glaze slurry and leave an even coat behind.

The second pass is the glaze firing, and it is taken considerably higher. Mid-range studio work is commonly fired to cone 6, approximately 1222 degrees Celsius, while traditional high-fire stoneware and porcelain go to cone 10, around 1285 degrees Celsius. At these temperatures the clay body vitrifies. Fluxes in the body melt and form a glassy phase that fills the pores between particles, and the piece becomes dense, strong, and nearly impermeable. Porcelain fired properly to cone 10 has water absorption below one percent and will ring when struck.

The term cone deserves explanation because it is routinely misunderstood. Orton pyrometric cones are small pyramids formulated to soften and bend at specific points, and what they actually measure is heat work rather than temperature. Heat work integrates temperature and time together, which matters because a kiln held at 1200 degrees for four hours does substantially more to a glaze than a kiln that touches 1200 degrees for ten minutes. This is why cone numbers, not thermocouple readings, define a firing. A pyrometer tells you where the kiln is at this instant; the cone tells you what the ware has actually experienced. Cone numbering is also counterintuitive: numbers with a zero prefix run cooler as the number rises, so cone 06 is cooler than cone 04, while unprefixed numbers run hotter as the number rises, so cone 10 is hotter than cone 6.

Several transformations along the way demand specific handling. Below about 200 degrees Celsius, residual atmospheric water leaves the clay. This stage is called water smoking, and rushing it is the most common cause of catastrophic failure in a bisque load, because trapped steam generates internal pressure faster than the porous body can vent it and the piece explodes. Between roughly 300 and 800 degrees, organic matter and carbon burn out, a process that requires oxygen and therefore adequate ventilation; carbon trapped past this window causes black coring and bloating later in the firing.

The most notorious threshold is quartz inversion at 573 degrees Celsius. Free silica in the clay body abruptly changes crystal structure from alpha to beta quartz, and the accompanying volume change of about one percent happens almost instantaneously. The transformation occurs on both the way up and the way down, which means every firing crosses it twice. Ramping too quickly through this window, particularly during cooling, produces dunting, a characteristic clean crack that often runs straight through a foot ring. A second and more subtle transition, the cristobalite inversion, occurs near 226 degrees Celsius during cooling in bodies that have developed cristobalite at high temperature, and it is a frequent culprit when pieces crack in a kiln that seems safely cool.

Atmosphere is the other great variable, and it is what most sharply divides electric from fuel-burning kilns. An electric kiln heats by resistance elements and admits ambient air, producing an oxidation atmosphere in which metal oxides in glazes remain fully oxidized. A gas, wood, or oil kiln can be deliberately starved of air by adjusting the burners and damper so that combustion is incomplete. The resulting reduction atmosphere is rich in carbon monoxide, which is chemically hungry for oxygen and will strip it directly out of the metal oxides in both glaze and clay body.

The visual consequences are dramatic and are the reason many potters tolerate the greater difficulty of fuel firing. Iron provides the clearest example. In oxidation, iron oxide yields ambers, honey browns, and warm yellows. In reduction, the same iron in a celadon glaze produces the cool blue-greens that Song dynasty potters spent centuries refining. Copper is even more extreme: in oxidation it gives familiar greens and turquoises, while in reduction it produces the deep, luminous copper reds known as sang de boeuf, or oxblood, which are notoriously difficult to reproduce consistently. Reduction also darkens and speckles the exposed clay body itself, since iron impurities in the stoneware react as well.

Beyond the standard oxidation and reduction firings lies a family of atmospheric techniques in which material is introduced into the kiln chamber. In salt and soda firing, sodium chloride or sodium carbonate is thrown into the kiln near peak temperature, where it volatilizes and combines with silica in the clay surface to form a sodium-alumino-silicate glaze directly on the ware. The result is the characteristic orange-peel texture, and because the vapor travels, the glaze reaches surfaces no brush could touch. Wood firing in a long chambered anagama kiln can run for several days of continuous stoking, during which flying ash settles on the pots and melts into a natural ash glaze whose pattern records the path the flame took through the chamber.

Raku is the outlier, and it inverts the usual relationship with time. Pieces are pulled from the kiln glowing at around 1000 degrees Celsius using tongs and moved immediately into a container of combustible material such as sawdust or newspaper, which ignites on contact. Sealing the container starves the fire and creates an intense post-firing reduction that pulls oxygen out of the glaze surface, producing metallic lusters and crackle patterns, while unglazed areas turn deep carbon black. The thermal shock is severe and deliberate. Raku ware is porous, structurally weak, and not food safe, which places it firmly in the category of decorative rather than functional ceramics.

Cooling is a genuine part of the firing schedule rather than an afterthought. Many crystalline and matte glazes require a controlled cooling cycle, sometimes involving a deliberate hold of several hours in the 1050 to 950 degree range, because the crystals that create the surface effect grow during cooling and simply do not form if the kiln is allowed to drop freely. Conversely, glossy glazes that are cooled too slowly can devitrify and turn cloudy. A common rule of thumb is that a kiln should not be opened until it has fallen below 200 degrees Celsius, and preferably closer to 100, both to protect the ware from thermal shock and to protect the operator from a face full of superheated air.
