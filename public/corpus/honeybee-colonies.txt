Inside the Honeybee Colony

A honeybee colony is best understood as a single organism distributed across tens of thousands of bodies. The western honeybee, Apis mellifera, does not survive as an individual in any meaningful sense. A bee separated from its colony dies within days. Decisions that look like intelligence, including where to forage, when to swarm, and how warm to keep the nest, emerge from the interaction of many simple individuals rather than from any central authority. The queen, despite her name, issues no orders.

A healthy colony at the height of summer contains somewhere between 20,000 and 80,000 bees. That population collapses in autumn to perhaps 10,000 as the colony conserves resources for winter. Three castes divide the labor. A single queen handles reproduction. Female workers, who are functionally sterile, perform every other task. A few hundred male drones exist during the reproductive season for the sole purpose of mating, and they are expelled from the hive to die when nectar flows end.

The queen's output during peak season is extraordinary. She can lay up to 2,000 eggs in a single day, a mass exceeding her own body weight. She mates only once in her life, during a series of nuptial flights in her first weeks, storing sperm from ten to twenty drones in an organ called the spermatheca. That single store must last her entire productive life of two to five years. Crucially, she controls the sex of each egg at the moment of laying. Fertilized eggs become diploid females, either workers or queens. Unfertilized eggs develop into haploid drones, meaning a drone has a grandfather but no father.

Development time differs by caste and is a useful diagnostic for beekeepers inspecting brood. A queen emerges 16 days after the egg is laid, a worker at 21 days, and a drone at 24 days. All larvae are fed royal jelly for their first three days. What determines whether a female larva becomes a worker or a queen is simply that queen larvae continue receiving royal jelly throughout development, while worker larvae are switched to a diet of pollen and honey. The difference is entirely nutritional, not genetic.

A worker's life is organized as a progression of jobs tied to her age, a pattern called temporal polyethism. For the first two or three days after emerging she cleans cells. From roughly day three to day eleven she serves as a nurse bee, feeding larvae from her hypopharyngeal glands. In the middle of her life she receives nectar from returning foragers, builds comb with wax secreted from glands on her abdomen, and stores and packs pollen. Around day eighteen she takes up guard duty at the entrance. Only in the final phase, from roughly day twenty-two onward, does she become a forager. A summer worker lives about six weeks, and much of that lifespan is consumed by the punishing work of foraging. Winter bees, which do not forage and carry heavier fat reserves, can live four to six months.

Foraging is a demanding calculation. A forager will typically visit between 50 and 100 flowers in a single trip and returns with roughly 40 milligrams of nectar, close to half her body weight. She exhibits flower constancy, meaning she tends to work a single species during a foraging bout, which is precisely the behavior that makes honeybees such efficient pollinators. The often-quoted figure is that producing a single pound of honey requires visiting roughly two million flowers and flying a cumulative distance comparable to circling the globe.

The mechanism by which foragers share location information is among the most remarkable communication systems in the animal kingdom. Karl von Frisch decoded it in the mid twentieth century and received a Nobel Prize for the work in 1973. A returning forager performs a waggle dance on the vertical face of the comb in the dark interior of the hive. She runs forward while vibrating her abdomen from side to side, then loops back to repeat the run, alternating left and right loops to trace a figure eight.

Two variables encode the message. The angle of the waggle run relative to vertical corresponds to the angle of the food source relative to the sun's current azimuth. A run performed straight up the comb means fly directly toward the sun; a run sixty degrees to the right of vertical means fly sixty degrees to the right of the sun. The duration of the waggle portion encodes distance, with roughly one second of waggling corresponding to approximately one kilometer of flight, though the precise calibration varies between subspecies. Bees watching in complete darkness read the dance by touch and sound. Remarkably, dancers compensate for the sun's movement across the sky, adjusting the angle of their dance over time even when they have been inside the hive for hours.

Temperature regulation is another colony-level achievement. The brood nest is held between 34 and 35 degrees Celsius with a precision of about half a degree, and this stability matters enormously: brood reared even slightly outside that band emerges with impaired learning ability and shortened lifespan. When the nest is too cool, workers decouple their wings from the flight muscles and shiver, generating metabolic heat. When it is too hot, they fan at the entrance to drive airflow and spread droplets of water on the comb so that evaporation carries heat away. In winter the colony forms a tight cluster around the queen, with bees continuously rotating between the insulating outer mantle and the warmer core, keeping the center near 20 degrees Celsius even when the outside air drops well below freezing.

Honey production is fundamentally a preservation problem. Nectar arrives at roughly 70 to 80 percent water, a concentration at which yeasts and bacteria would thrive. Workers reduce it below 18 percent by repeatedly exposing droplets to hive air and by fanning to accelerate evaporation. Simultaneously, the enzyme invertase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, and glucose oxidase generates small quantities of hydrogen peroxide. The resulting product is so hypertonic that it draws water out of any microbial cell that lands in it. Once the moisture target is met, workers cap the cell with wax, and the honey becomes effectively immortal. Sealed jars recovered from Egyptian tombs after three thousand years have been found still edible.

Reproduction at the colony level occurs through swarming. When a colony becomes crowded in late spring, workers construct queen cups and the old queen departs with roughly half the workforce, leaving a developing daughter behind. The swarm settles in a temporary cluster while a few hundred scout bees inspect potential cavities and advertise their findings with waggle dances. Scouts that visit a rival site and find it superior will switch their allegiance, and the cluster reaches a decision when one site accumulates a quorum of committed scouts. The entire process is a distributed vote, resolved without any individual ever comparing all the options.
