Affinage: The Ripening of Cheese

Making cheese and ripening cheese are two different crafts, and in much of Europe they have historically been done by different people. The maker produces a young wheel; the affineur takes it and, over weeks to years, turns it into something else entirely. Almost everything a person recognises as the character of a cheese is created during ripening rather than at the point of manufacture.

The young wheel arrives as a dense, bland, rubbery mass of protein and fat with very little flavour. It contains three sets of tools that will do the work. There are the starter cultures added at the beginning, lactic acid bacteria that converted milk sugar into lactic acid and then largely died, leaving their enzymes behind. There is residual rennet, the coagulating enzyme, which continues slowly cutting protein chains. And there are whatever secondary organisms the affineur chooses to encourage on the surface.

Ripening is fundamentally the breakdown of large molecules into small ones. Proteolysis cuts the protein network into peptides and then into free amino acids, which is what turns a squeaky curd into something that yields and finally becomes creamy. Lipolysis breaks fats into free fatty acids, producing the sharp and sometimes goaty notes that dominate in aged sheep and goat cheeses. Both processes run continuously, and their relative speed is what an affineur manipulates.

The controls available are few and blunt. Temperature in a ripening cave typically sits between ten and thirteen degrees Celsius, which is warm enough that enzymes work but cool enough that they work slowly and evenly. Humidity is held high, commonly between eighty-five and ninety-five percent relative humidity, because a wheel that dries too fast forms a hard skin that stops moisture leaving the interior and arrests ripening in the middle. Air movement matters as much as either: stagnant air encourages the wrong moulds, and excessive draught dries the rind.

Different families of cheese depend on entirely different surface ecologies. A bloomy rind, as on Brie and Camembert, is Penicillium camemberti, a white mould deliberately introduced that grows a felt across the surface and ripens the cheese from the outside inward. This is why a young Camembert has a chalky core and a properly ripe one is uniformly soft; the softening front is moving inward from the rind, and cutting it early reveals exactly how far the process has travelled.

A washed rind takes a different route. The wheel is wiped regularly with brine, sometimes with beer or wine, which suppresses moulds and instead favours Brevibacterium linens, the orange bacterium responsible for the pungency of Époisses and Munster. The same organism lives on human skin, which accounts for the widely noted resemblance. Washed-rind cheeses need more frequent attention than any other kind, sometimes twice weekly for months.

The hard alpine cheeses take the longest and change the most. A wheel of Comté or Gruyère is pressed and brined, then ripened for anything from four months to three years. Over that time proteolysis is slow but relentless, and after a year or more the free amino acid tyrosine begins to crystallise. Those crystals are the small white crunchy specks in a well-aged wheel, and they are a reliable indicator of genuine age rather than a defect. A second kind of crystal, calcium lactate, forms differently and appears on the surface rather than within the paste.

Blue cheeses invert the usual geometry. Penicillium roqueforti is mixed into the curd rather than applied to the surface, but it requires oxygen to grow, so the wheel is pierced with needles after a few weeks to admit air along the channels. The blue veining follows those piercings exactly, which is why the pattern in a wheel of Stilton is regular rather than random.

Turning is the affineur's most repetitive and least negotiable duty. A wheel left sitting collects moisture on its underside, ripens unevenly, and develops a flattened damp face where unwanted organisms establish themselves. Depending on the style, a wheel may be turned daily at first and weekly later, and a cave holding several thousand wheels represents a very great deal of lifting.

Knowing when a cheese is ready is not a matter of counting days. Affineurs judge by feel, pressing the wheel to gauge how far the paste has softened, and by sound, tapping or striking it and listening for the dull note of a solid interior against the hollow one that indicates a crack or a gas pocket. The most experienced use an iron, a narrow probe pushed into the wheel to extract a core, which is smelled and tasted and then pressed carefully back into place.
