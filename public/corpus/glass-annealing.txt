Glassblowing and the Annealing Problem

Glass is a liquid that has been cooled until it stopped flowing, without ever arranging itself into a crystal. That single fact governs everything about working it. Because there is no crystal structure to break down, glass has no melting point in the way that a metal does. It has instead a continuous range of viscosity, and every operation a glassblower performs is defined by where on that range the material currently sits.

At around one thousand degrees Celsius soda-lime glass is at its working point, fluid enough to gather on a pipe and shape with tools but still coherent enough to hold a form. As it cools it stiffens continuously rather than setting at a threshold, which is why a blower works in short bursts and returns constantly to the reheating chamber. Near seven hundred degrees the glass reaches its softening point, where it will still slump under its own weight but can no longer be blown. Below that it is dimensionally stable and, to the eye, finished.

It is not finished. Glass cooled from working temperature to room temperature without intervention will almost always break, sometimes on the bench and sometimes weeks later on a shelf. The cause is that glass conducts heat poorly. The surface of a cooling piece contracts and rigidifies while the interior is still hot and mobile. When the interior finally cools and tries to contract, the already-rigid surface will not let it, and the piece is left holding permanent internal stress. Sufficient stress makes the object break spontaneously, and pieces that fail this way often come apart with no impact at all.

Annealing is the controlled cooling that removes that stress, and it is defined by two specific temperatures. The annealing point, for typical soda-lime glass somewhere between five hundred and ten and five hundred and sixty degrees, is the temperature at which internal stress relaxes within minutes because the glass is still just mobile enough to flow at the molecular scale. The strain point, roughly forty to sixty degrees lower, is where that mobility effectively stops. Between those two temperatures a piece can be relieved of stress; below the strain point, nothing further can be relieved, but nothing new can be introduced either, provided cooling from there is gentle.

The practical consequence is that annealing has two distinct phases. The piece is held at or just above the annealing point long enough for stress to relax throughout its thickness, which for a thin blown vessel may be twenty minutes and for a thick solid sculpture may be many hours. It is then cooled slowly and evenly through the strain point, after which the rate can increase considerably. The oven that does this is called a lehr, and in a working studio it runs continuously, accepting finished pieces throughout the day and cooling overnight on a programmed schedule.

Thickness dominates the schedule far more than size does. A large thin bowl anneals faster than a small solid paperweight, because what matters is how far heat must travel to escape from the centre. Rules of thumb in the trade scale annealing time with roughly the square of thickness, which is why a paperweight that looks trivial can require a schedule of a day or more while a delicate goblet is safe overnight.

The stress that annealing removes can be seen directly. Viewed through crossed polarising filters, a stressed piece shows bright coloured fringes where the glass is birefringent under strain, and a properly annealed piece appears nearly uniform. Studios keep a polariscope for exactly this check, since the alternative is discovering the problem when a customer's vase fails.

Thermal expansion explains why glasses cannot be casually mixed. Every formulation has a coefficient of thermal expansion describing how much it grows when heated, and soda-lime glass expands roughly three times as much as borosilicate. Joining two glasses with different coefficients guarantees stress at the join as they cool at different rates, and the piece will eventually fail there. This is why studios standardise rigidly on one compatible family of colours and clear glass, and why borosilicate, with its low expansion, tolerates the thermal shock that makes it suitable for laboratory and cookware use.

One further hazard lies in the middle of the cooling range. Held too long at temperatures where crystals can nucleate, glass will begin to devitrify, growing crystalline regions that appear as a dull grey or milky skin on the surface. Devitrification is not a stress problem and annealing does not cause it, but a badly designed schedule that lingers in the wrong band will produce it. Once it has formed it cannot be reheated away; the affected surface must be ground off or the piece abandoned.
