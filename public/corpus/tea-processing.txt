How Tea Is Made

Green tea, oolong, black tea, and white tea are not different plants. All of them come from Camellia sinensis, and in most cases from the same varieties and often the same gardens. What separates them is entirely a matter of processing, and specifically of how far one enzymatic reaction is allowed to proceed before it is stopped with heat.

That reaction is oxidation, and it is worth naming carefully because the tea trade has called it fermentation for over a century and the term persists. Fermentation, properly, is microbial. What happens in black tea is not: it is an enzyme within the leaf itself, polyphenol oxidase, acting on the leaf's own catechins in the presence of oxygen. There is one genuine exception, discussed below, and it is the reason the confusion has survived so long.

Processing begins with withering. Fresh leaf arrives at roughly seventy-five to eighty percent moisture and is spread on troughs or racks, often with air drawn through them, for several hours and sometimes overnight. The leaf loses perhaps a fifth of its water and becomes limp enough to handle without shattering. Withering is not merely drying; the concentration of compounds inside the leaf changes as water leaves, and aroma precursors develop that will not appear if this stage is rushed.

Rolling comes next and is the step that starts the clock. Rolling, whether by hand or between machine plates, ruptures cell walls and brings polyphenol oxidase into contact with the catechins it will act on. Until this moment the enzyme and its substrate have been kept apart inside intact cells. From the instant the leaf is bruised, oxidation is running, and everything after this point is a decision about when to stop it.

Left to proceed, oxidation converts colourless catechins into theaflavins and then into the larger thearubigins. The leaf darkens from green through copper to brown, and the liquor it will brew shifts from pale and astringent toward deep, malty, and full-bodied. Black tea is oxidised essentially to completion, typically over forty-five minutes to two hours in a cool humid room around twenty-five to thirty degrees Celsius, with the maker judging progress by smell as much as by colour.

Stopping oxidation requires denaturing the enzyme, which means heat, and the step is universally called kill-green. Chinese practice traditionally pan-fires the leaf in a wok at roughly one hundred and fifty to two hundred degrees, tumbling it constantly for a few minutes; the brief contact with hot metal contributes a toasty note characteristic of many Chinese green teas. Japanese practice instead steams the leaf for fifteen to forty-five seconds, which halts oxidation faster and preserves a vivid green colour and a distinctly marine, vegetal character. The same plant, processed by these two methods, produces teas that taste almost nothing alike.

Green tea is simply tea in which kill-green happens almost immediately, before meaningful oxidation has occurred. Oolong is the deliberate middle ground, oxidised anywhere from ten to eighty percent before firing, and it is the most labour-intensive category to make. Oolong leaves are typically bruised repeatedly and gently, often by tossing in bamboo baskets, so that the leaf edges oxidise while the centres do not. A finished oolong leaf inspected closely shows a red rim around a still-green middle, which is a direct record of that selective bruising.

White tea is the least processed of all and, counterintuitively, is not unoxidised. It is withered and dried with no rolling and no kill-green, so a slow light oxidation occurs throughout without the enzyme ever being released in quantity. The result is delicate and sweet, and depends more on the quality of the raw leaf than any other style, since there is almost no processing to hide behind.

Firing, or drying, finishes every style. The leaf is brought down to around three percent moisture, usually in hot air dryers near one hundred and twenty degrees, which arrests all remaining activity and makes the tea stable for storage. Insufficient drying is the most common cause of tea spoiling in transit, since residual moisture allows moulds to establish.

The genuine exception is puerh, and it justifies the older vocabulary. After the leaf is fired, puerh is deliberately exposed to microbial action, with fungi including Aspergillus species and various bacteria acting on the leaf over months or years. Ripe puerh accelerates this by piling damp leaf under controlled warmth and humidity for weeks, while raw puerh is compressed and left to change slowly over decades. That is fermentation in the strict sense, driven by organisms rather than by the leaf's own enzymes, and it is the only branch of tea making for which the word is accurate.
