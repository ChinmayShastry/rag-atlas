The Hillside Workshop: A Fictional Profile

Marta Vance is an invented character, written for this demonstration. Nothing here describes a real person. Her profile exists because it deliberately withholds the facts it depends on: almost every specific in this document points at a number recorded somewhere else in the corpus, and never states that number itself.

Marta runs a small workshop on a north-facing hillside, where she divides her week between several crafts and declines to rank them.

Her pottery is stoneware, thrown on a kick wheel and fired in a gas kiln she built herself from a converted bread oven. She glazes for cone 10 and refuses to work any cooler, on the grounds that anything less does not properly vitrify and she dislikes the sound a half-matured pot makes when tapped. Her kiln is deliberately fuel-burning rather than electric because she wants the option of starving the burners of air. The copper glaze she is best known for, a deep and slightly variable red she calls Hillside Ox, only develops its colour under that starved atmosphere; fired in an electric kiln the same glaze comes out a flat and disappointing green. She has never used salt or soda in the chamber and considers wood firing a wonderful thing that other people should do.

Cooling is where she claims most potters lose their work. She holds her kiln for several hours on the way down through the crystalline range, because the matte surfaces she wants do not form if the kiln is allowed to fall freely, and she will not open the door early no matter how impatient the customer. Twice in her first year she cracked a whole shelf of work by rushing the cooling, and both times the fracture ran clean through the foot ring in the way that indicates the silica in the body changed structure faster than the pot could accommodate.

Behind the workshop she keeps two hives of Apis mellifera, which she took up after reading that a colony makes decisions the way a good workshop does, by distributed argument rather than instruction. She is fascinated by the fact that her queens control the sex of every egg at the moment of laying, and that the only thing separating a worker from a queen is what the larva is fed after its third day. In a good summer she does nothing to the hives at all beyond watching the entrance, and she can tell from the traffic there whether a nectar flow is on. She has never counted the eggs a queen lays in a day but has read the figure and finds it difficult to believe.

Her coffee is the part of the week she guards most jealously. She buys green beans from a single importer, always high-grown and dense, and roasts them in a one-kilo drum roaster in batches small enough to finish in about eleven minutes. She takes her roasts to first crack and drops them within ninety seconds of hearing it start, which puts her firmly in light-roast territory and produces the bright, tea-like cup she prefers. She has no interest in reaching second crack and describes the flavour of anything taken that far as "a smell, not a taste." Because she roasts light, her weight loss is at the low end of the range, and she weighs every batch before and after to confirm it.

She will not brew a roast on the day she makes it. Her filter coffee sits for the better part of a week before she touches it, and the espresso she pulls on the workshop machine waits considerably longer than that, because she has found that the pressure of an espresso shot exposes under-rested coffee in a way that a filter brew forgives. Everything is stored in valved bags, and she grinds immediately before brewing, which she regards as the least negotiable rule in the building.

A jar of starter lives on the shelf above the roaster, fed on the same schedule for eleven years. She keeps it deliberately cool and stiff rather than warm and slack, because she wants the sharper of the two acids a starter can produce, and she is dismissive of bakers who chase sourness by extending the bulk ferment instead. Her loaves go into a domestic oven she considers inadequate and are pulled when the internal temperature reaches the figure she was taught, which she checks with a probe rather than by tapping.

In the outbuilding she keeps an indigo vat, a fermentation vat rather than a chemical one, fed with madder and bran and held at the warm end of its working range by a heater she salvaged from an aquarium. She judges its health by the coppery bloom on the surface and by the colour of the liquid beneath, and she will not dye protein fibres in it at all, on the grounds that the alkalinity required would ruin them. The linen she dyes goes through twelve dips.

The one tool she did not make is a set of chisels, and she has strong views about their heat treatment. She retempers them herself when they chip, taking them to the pale straw colour rather than anything darker, and accepts that they will chip again rather than lose the edge retention.
