Sourdough: Fermentation as a Managed Ecosystem

A sourdough starter is not a single organism but a small stable ecosystem, and understanding it as such explains most of what a baker can and cannot control. Two populations live in the flour and water paste. Wild yeasts, commonly species of Kazachstania and Saccharomyces, consume sugars and release carbon dioxide, which is what lifts the dough. Lactic acid bacteria, chiefly of the Lactobacillus and Fructilactobacillus groups, produce the acids that give the bread its flavour and its keeping quality. In a mature starter the bacteria outnumber the yeasts by roughly a hundred to one, and the acidity they create is precisely what keeps competing organisms out.

A starter is maintained by discarding most of it and feeding the remainder equal weights of flour and water, a ratio usually written as one to one to one. The discard is not waste in any biological sense; it exists because a population fed on a fixed amount of flour must be kept small enough that the food is not exhausted before the baker wants to use it. A starter left unfed becomes progressively more acidic as the bacteria continue working, and eventually the acidity suppresses the yeasts, at which point the culture rises poorly and smells sharply of solvent.

Hydration is the single number bakers argue about most. It is the weight of water divided by the weight of flour, expressed as a percentage, and it governs how the dough behaves at every stage. A stiff dough at sixty-five percent holds its shape and is easy to handle but produces a tighter crumb. A slack dough at eighty-five percent flows, sticks, and demands more skill, but yields the open irregular holes that many bakers are chasing. Hydration also changes fermentation speed, because a wetter dough allows the organisms to move and feed more freely.

The two acids produced are not interchangeable, and the balance between them is under the baker's control. Lactic acid is soft and yoghurt-like. Acetic acid is sharp and vinegary. Warm fermentation, above roughly twenty-six degrees Celsius, and higher hydration both favour lactic acid, producing a mild loaf. Cool fermentation and stiffer dough favour acetic acid and a distinctly sour one. A baker who wants more sourness does not add anything; they lower the temperature and stiffen the dough.

Gluten development is a separate process running alongside fermentation. Flour contains two proteins, glutenin and gliadin, which on contact with water link into the elastic network that traps the gas the yeast produces. Many bakers begin with an autolyse, mixing only flour and water and leaving it to rest for twenty to sixty minutes before adding starter and salt. During that rest the flour hydrates fully and enzymes begin cutting the proteins, so the dough reaches the same strength with far less mechanical work.

Bulk fermentation is where the dough actually becomes bread, and it is judged by volume and feel rather than by the clock. At twenty-four to twenty-six degrees it commonly runs four to six hours. During this period the baker performs a series of folds rather than continuous kneading, each fold stretching the gluten network and redistributing the gas into finer bubbles. The dough is ready when it has risen by perhaps half again its original volume, feels aerated, and holds a domed shape when tipped.

Many bakers then retard the shaped loaf in a refrigerator between four and twelve degrees for twelve to eighteen hours. Retarding does several things at once. Yeast activity slows almost to a stop while bacterial activity continues, so the loaf grows more sour without over-proofing. The cold dough is also far easier to score cleanly, and the slow rise develops flavour compounds that a fast warm ferment never produces.

Baking is a sequence of rapid transformations. The loaf goes into an oven at around two hundred and thirty to two hundred and fifty degrees Celsius. In the first ten minutes the trapped gas expands and the remaining yeast makes a final burst of carbon dioxide before dying, producing the sudden rise known as oven spring. Steam in the oven during this period keeps the surface flexible so the loaf can expand; without it the crust sets early and the bread tears at its weakest point instead of at the score.

Once the steam is vented the crust begins to colour. Two reactions dominate, both requiring heat and the absence of surface water. The Maillard reaction couples amino acids with reducing sugars, and caramelisation breaks sugars down under heat alone. Together they produce the brown colour and the great majority of the aroma, which is why an underbaked loaf tastes flat regardless of how well it was fermented. The bread is finished when its internal temperature reaches roughly ninety-six to ninety-nine degrees, at which point the starch has fully gelatinised.

Cooling is part of baking rather than something that happens afterwards. A loaf cut straight from the oven is gummy, because the starch is still setting and steam is still leaving the crumb. An hour of cooling on a rack lets that finish. Cutting early does not merely release steam; it permanently changes the texture of the slice.
